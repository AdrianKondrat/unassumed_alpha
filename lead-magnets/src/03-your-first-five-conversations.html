UNASSUMED.01 · Find ten people

Start warm. Cold is the last resort.

From first principles: people say yes when they already care about the problem, or when someone they trust asks them to. Founders who share their outreach stories publicly tend to report the same thing: cold messages mostly go unanswered, while warm intros and people already complaining get replies. So work left to right.

WARMESTAlready complainingPeople posting about this problem in forums, reviews, groups. Reply helpfully first. Check each community’s rules on self-promotion.
WARMOne intro awayAsk friends, ex-colleagues, classmates: “Who do you know who has dealt with ___ recently?”
MILDWhere they gatherMeetups, associations, Slack and Discord groups, courses where your segment already spends time.
COOLYour own pastPeople from old jobs, clients, suppliers who fit the segment, even loosely.
COLDStrangersShort, specific, no pitch. Expect silence; send more than you need.

Why five, and why ten names? Five conversations won’t prove anything. They tell you whether you’re talking to the right people and asking the right questions. Research on interview saturation suggests recurring themes start to repeat after roughly a dozen or so interviews in a fairly similar group, and later when the group is mixed. Ten names gets you five calls if half say yes. Guest, Bunce & Johnson (2006), Field Methods 18(1); Hagaman & Wutich (2016), Field Methods.

UNASSUMED.02 · Three messages that get a yes

Ask for their story, not your validation.

People like being asked about their own experience. They don’t enjoy being asked to judge your idea. Fill in the blanks, keep it short, and send it today.

A · The warm-intro ask

To a friend

Hi , quick favour. I’m trying to understand how deal with . Do you know anyone who’s had to deal with that in the last few months? I’d love 20 minutes to hear how they handle it. I’m not selling anything.

Why it works: you’re asking for an introduction, not a sale, and “in the last few months” filters for people with fresh, specific memories.

B · The reply to someone who complained

Public post → DM

Hi , I saw your post about . That sounds painful. I’m researching how people handle this and I’m not selling anything. Would you be up for a 20-minute chat about what happened and what you tried? Happy to share what I learn from others.

Why it works: they’ve already told the world it’s a problem. Lead with their words, and offer something back (a summary of what you learn).

C · The cold email

Last resort

Subject: Quick question about
Hi , I’m researching how handle , and your work at suggests you’ve seen it up close. Could I ask you about the last time it came up? 20 minutes, your choice of time, and no pitch. If it’s not a fit, a one-line “no” is genuinely fine.

Why it works: it’s specific about why you chose them, it gives them permission to say no, and it promises no pitch. Then keep that promise.

Never in a first message

“Validate”It tells them you want a yes.
A deck or a linkIt turns a chat into a review.
“Would you use…”Save it. Better still, never ask it.
An hourAsk for 20 minutes and finish on time.

UNASSUMED.03 · The 30-minute run sheet

Thirty minutes, in four parts.

Print this and keep it beside you on the call. Write in the margins. Change it after every conversation.

0–3′SET-UP
Lower the stakes. “I’m not selling anything. I’m trying to understand how people deal with ___. There are no wrong answers, and specific stories help me most.”
3–18′THE LAST TIME
One real event, in detail.
  • “Tell me about the last time ___ happened.”
  • “What happened just before that? And after?”
  • “What did you try? What did it cost you, in time or money?”
  • “Who else was involved?”
18–25′THEIR WORLD
Zoom out, still about them.
  • “What’s the hardest part about ___?”
  • “Have you looked for a better way? What happened?”
  • “How does this rank against everything else on your plate?”
25–30′CLOSE
Ask for one next step.
  • “Who else should I talk to about this?”
  • “Can I come back to you once I’ve spoken to a few more people?”

Openers adapted from Y Combinator’s “How to Talk to Users” and Rob Fitzpatrick’s The Mom Test.

When it goes sideways

They say

“Oh, I’d totally use something like that.”

You say

“Thanks! What are you using today? What did it cost you last time?”

They start designing your product

“You should add a feature that…”

You say

“Interesting. Has that come up for you? When was the last time?”

You catch yourself pitching

You’ve been talking for two minutes straight.

You do

Stop mid-sentence. “Sorry, I’m talking too much. Tell me about the last time…”

They go vague

“Usually I just sort of deal with it.”

You say

“Could you walk me through the most recent time, step by step?”
